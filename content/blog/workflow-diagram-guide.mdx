# Workflow Diagram: Symbols, Examples & How to Make One

A workflow diagram shows how work actually moves: who does what, in what order, and what happens when something needs approval, fails a check, or gets handed to another team. Teams draw them to onboard new people, find bottlenecks, and agree on a process before automating it.

This guide covers the symbols worth knowing, eight **workflow diagram examples** you can copy, swimlane diagrams, and the fastest way to turn a written process into a finished diagram.

## What is a workflow diagram?

A workflow diagram is a visual map of a repeatable process from trigger to completion. Unlike a simple checklist, it captures three things a list can't: **sequence** (what follows what), **decisions** (where the path splits), and **ownership** (who is responsible for each step). If your process has ever failed because "I thought marketing was handling that," a workflow diagram is the fix.

## Workflow diagram symbols

You only need a handful of shapes, and consistency matters more than the exact convention:

- **Oval** — start and end points (the trigger and the outcome).
- **Rectangle** — a task or action someone performs.
- **Diamond** — a decision with labeled branches (usually yes/no).
- **Arrow** — the direction work flows.
- **Parallelogram** — an input or output, like a submitted form or a generated report.
- **Swimlane** — a horizontal or vertical band grouping steps by owner (more below).

Modern tools relax the strict symbol rules and use icons and color instead — what matters is that readers can spot tasks, decisions, and owners at a glance. Our guide to [flowchart symbols](/blog/flowchart-symbols-guide) covers the classic notation in depth.

## Swimlane workflow diagrams

A swimlane diagram is a workflow diagram divided into lanes, one per owner — for example Marketing / Sales / Finance, or Customer / Support / Engineering. Every task sits in the lane of the person or team responsible, so handoffs become visible as arrows crossing lane boundaries.

Use swimlanes when the pain point is coordination between teams rather than the steps themselves. Crossing arrows are the tell: each one is a handoff that can drop, stall, or duplicate work. If one lane's tasks constantly ping-pong with another's, that's usually where the process needs redesign — or automation.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is the difference between a workflow diagram and a flowchart?**

A flowchart is the general format — boxes and arrows for any logic. A workflow diagram is a flowchart applied specifically to a repeatable business process, and it usually adds ownership (who does each step), often via swimlanes.

**What are the main workflow diagram symbols?**

Ovals for start/end, rectangles for tasks, diamonds for decisions, arrows for flow, and swimlanes for ownership. Most modern diagrams add icons and color on top of (or instead of) strict symbol notation.

**What is the best workflow diagram maker?**

It depends on where your process lives. If it's already written down — in a doc, a ticket, an SOP — a text-to-diagram tool is fastest: paste the description and the diagram generates itself. If you want to design visually from scratch, a classic drag-and-drop editor gives more manual control at the cost of layout time.

**When should I use a swimlane diagram instead?**

When more than two owners touch the process. Swimlanes make cross-team handoffs — the usual source of delays — visible as lane-crossing arrows.
